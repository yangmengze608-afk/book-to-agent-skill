# Principles

> Core principles from The Decision Notebook. Each entry carries its
> content type and source provenance. Ids are referenced from SKILL.md.

### P1. State every significant decision as a bet
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 1, section "Every Decision Is a Bet"
- source_confidence: high

Treat each choice as a wager on beliefs against an uncertain future. A bet
has three inspectable parts: the belief it rests on, the stake, and the odds
implicitly accepted. Beginners look only at the stake; practitioners write
all three before choosing. If you cannot say "I am betting that __", the
decision is not yet understood.

### P2. Never judge a decision by its outcome alone (no resulting)
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 2, section "Resulting: The Trap of Judging by Outcomes"
- source_confidence: high

Separate two questions that feel like one: was the decision well made, and
did it turn out well? The four-cell table (decision quality x outcome
quality) exists because outcome is visible while decision quality is not.
The most dangerous cell is a badly made decision that succeeded: it rewards
and teaches the wrong process.

### P3. Demand sample size before drawing conclusions
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 2, section "Resulting: The Trap of Judging by Outcomes"
- source_confidence: high

A small number of outcomes is compatible with skill, with luck, and with a
coin that has not yet shown its bias. Three wins in a row is not evidence of
a validated method; a track record over many trials is.

### P4. Confidence is a number with a date
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 3, section "Updating: Hearing the Crowd Correctly"
- source_confidence: high

Beliefs are working estimates. Attach numerical confidence ("80% this
launch slips two weeks"), date it, and let the numbers accumulate into a
calibration record. Numbers can be scored; vibes cannot. A single outcome
falsifies almost no probabilistic claim - a 70% rain forecast is wrong in
neither rain nor sunshine on any one day; only hundreds of 70%-days settle
it.

### P5. Update beliefs in proportion to surprise
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 3, section "Updating: Hearing the Crowd Correctly"
- source_confidence: high

Evidence already expected should barely move a belief; surprising evidence
should move it a lot. Before updating, ask: was this information already
priced in? The two failure modes are the stubborn (never update, treat every
surprise as noise) and the weather-vane (update on noise as if it were
signal). State what would have moved you more, and what would not have
moved you at all.

### P6. Size stakes to evidence; never bet what you cannot survive
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 5, section "Sizing: Bold When Right, Careful When Unsure"
- source_confidence: high

Stake in proportion to your edge, never in proportion to your excitement.
With thin evidence, make the bet small enough that being wrong is cheap
tuition; size up only gradually even for strong beliefs. Name the worst
realistic case first: if it is unrecoverable, the probability of gain does
not matter - no expected value calculation rescues a bet you cannot afford
to lose. Diversify across independent bets, not across ten copies of one
bet wearing costumes.

### P7. Externalize decisions before outcomes are known
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 4, section "The Decision Journal"
- source_confidence: high

Memory rebuilds the past to fit the present ("I thought" becomes "I knew").
Record decisions in a journal before any outcome arrives; entries written
afterward are memoirs, not instruments. The journal converts vague regret
into specific lessons and protects against group hindsight ("we all knew").

### P8. Pre-commit exit conditions while still objective
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 6, section "Premortems and Tripwires"
- source_confidence: high

Before committing, run a premortem that names failure modes; after
committing, write tripwires (condition + date + pre-agreed action) that
convert "keep an eye on it" into an actual decision trigger. Tripwires
defend against escalation of commitment and status-quo drift, and they are
strongest when written by the pre-commitment self.

### P9. Prospective hindsight surfaces more failure modes
- type: EVIDENCE
- source: chapter 6, section "Premortems and Tripwires"
- source_confidence: medium

The book cites research on prospective hindsight: people generate more
failure modes when they assume the failure has already happened than when
they imagine it as a possibility. The premortem's past-tense framing is
therefore functional, not stylistic. (The book describes this finding
without naming the study; treat the mechanism as reported, not verified.)

### P10. Adoption pace: one practice at a time
- type: AUTHOR CLAIM
- source: chapter 8, section "The Practicing Decider"
- source_confidence: high

Do not adopt the whole notebook at once. Adopt one practice (the decision
journal is the usual first), run it for a month, and let the practices prove
themselves - that, too, is a bet, and a cheap one.

### P11. Decision-skill mapping for agents
- type: DISTILLER INFERENCE
- derived_from: P1, P2, P4, P6

When an agent applies this skill: restate the user's situation as a bet,
score decision quality and outcome quality on separate axes when reviewing,
express every confidence numerically, and check survivability before
endorsing any commitment size. If the user's question lacks a stake, ask for
it before proceeding.
